# Content Plan — @athenahealtheducation

28 quote cards, five pillars, eight weeks at three posts a week. Every card drives to fragilityfracture.ie.

## The strategy in one paragraph

Osteoporosis has no symptoms, so nobody searches for it and nobody follows an account about it. What people do react to is a number that contradicts what they assumed. That is what this account has that nobody else in the Irish market has: 400,000 people, €460 million, 74% untreated at first fracture, and Athena's own 2023 research on 600 Irish adults. We lead with the number, we make the human point in one line under it, and we send them to the risk checker. We are not trying to teach osteoporosis on Instagram. We are trying to get someone to ask their GP one question.

## Pillars

| # | Pillar | Cards | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | The Silent Epidemic | 7 | Scale. Make it feel Irish and common, not rare and foreign. |
| B | Know a Fragility Fracture | 5 | Recognition. Teach the one thing that makes someone act: a break from a small fall is not bad luck. |
| C | Calcium & Vitamin D | 9 | Practical. The gap between what people take and what they need. |
| D | Test & Treat | 4 | Conversion. Turn awareness into a GP question or a risk check. |
| E | Build & Protect | 3 | Hope. It's not too late, and here's what works. |

The mix is deliberate: A and B earn the reach, C keeps people following, D converts. Never post two D cards in the same week; the account stops feeling like education and starts feeling like an ad.

### B — Know a Fragility Fracture

**B1-what-is-it.png** — Quote, cream. Definition
> A fragility fracture is a broken bone caused by a force that would not normally break a healthy bone. A minor fall. A bump against a door frame. Sometimes just an everyday movement.
>
> Healthy bone is built to take that. When a small force produces a break, the force isn't the story. The bone is.
>
> Full explainer at fragilityfracture.ie — link in bio.

**B2-first-sign.png** — Quote, dark. First visible sign
> A fragility fracture is often the first visible sign of osteoporosis.
>
> Which is a strange thing to sit with. The condition has already been progressing for years by the time you find out about it, and the way you find out is that something breaks.
>
> That's exactly why testing matters. A DXA scan can see the bone loss while it's still just bone loss.

**B3-sites.png** — Sites list, white. Hip, spine, wrist, upper arm
> Fragility fractures happen most often at four sites: the hip, the spine, the wrist, and the upper arm or shoulder.
>
> The wrist is often the first, because it's what you land on. The spine is often the quietest — some spinal fractures cause no obvious pain and show up only as lost height or a change in posture.
>
> A break at any of these after a minor fall deserves a bone health conversation, whatever your age.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie & International Osteoporosis Foundation.

**B4-men.png** — Stat, dark. 1 in 5 men
> One in five men over 50 will have an osteoporosis-related fracture.
>
> Osteoporosis is not a women's disease. It's a disease that is diagnosed like one. Men are less likely to be screened, less likely to be tested after a fracture, and less likely to be treated when they are diagnosed.
>
> If you're a man over 50 who has broken a bone from a small fall, that's a reason to ask about your bone health.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

**B5-menopause.png** — Stat, cream. 20%
> Women can lose up to 20% of their bone density in the first five to seven years after menopause.
>
> Oestrogen protects bone. When it drops, the rate of loss accelerates sharply, and it does it in a defined window — which is unusual, and useful, because it means you know when to pay attention.
>
> If you're approaching or going through menopause, this is the conversation to have with your GP now rather than at 70.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

### C — Calcium & Vitamin D

**C1-99pc.png** — Stat, white. 99%
> 99% of the calcium in your body is stored in your skeleton.
>
> Your bones aren't just the frame that holds you up. They're the reserve your body draws on when your blood calcium runs low, because your heart and nerves need calcium and they get first call on it.
>
> Which is the whole reason diet matters here. Keep the reserve topped up and your body doesn't have to withdraw from it.

**C2-borrows.png** — Quote, yellow. Your body takes it from your bones
> When you don't eat enough calcium, your body takes it from your bones.
>
> Not as a punishment. As housekeeping. Blood calcium has to stay in a narrow range or your heart and nerves stop working properly, so if the diet doesn't supply it, the skeleton does.
>
> Do that for thirty years and you get osteoporosis. There's no point at which it feels like anything is happening.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

**C3-vitd-partner.png** — Quote, cream. Calcium needs Vitamin D
> Calcium can't do its job without Vitamin D.
>
> Vitamin D is what allows your gut to absorb the calcium you eat. Without enough of it, a good diet still leaves you short, because the calcium goes through you rather than into you.
>
> This is why so many people are surprised by a diagnosis. They were doing the calcium part. They just weren't absorbing it.

**C4-1200mg.png** — Stat, dark. 1,200mg
> Women aged 51 and over need 1,200mg of calcium a day.
>
> For reference: adults 19 to 50 need 1,000mg, and everyone over 70 needs 1,200mg.
>
> 1,200mg is roughly four servings of dairy. If dairy isn't part of your diet, it's a number you have to build deliberately from fortified milks, tinned fish with the bones in, calcium-set tofu, and greens.
>
> Talk to your GP or pharmacist about what your intake should be. Sources: International Osteoporosis Foundation 2025, Institute of Medicine 2011.

**C5-gap.png** — Stat, cream. 600–700mg
> Irish adults over 50 average 600 to 700mg of calcium a day. The target is 1,200mg.
>
> That's 54% of what's needed. The other 46% doesn't come from nowhere. It comes out of your bones, quietly, for decades.
>
> This gap is the most fixable thing in Irish bone health, and it's the one almost nobody knows they have.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

**C6-76pc.png** — Stat, white. 76%
> 76% of Irish adults over 40 don't get the recommended amount of calcium in their diet.
>
> That figure is from research Athena commissioned in 2023, surveying 600 Irish men and women over 40. It's not a global average borrowed from somewhere else. It's us.
>
> Three quarters of Irish adults over 40 are running a calcium deficit and have no idea. If you want to know where you stand, start by adding up yesterday.
>
> Source: Research commissioned by Athena, conducted by Empathy Research, 2023.

**C7-dairy.png** — Quote, cream. Dairy
> Dairy is the richest and most readily absorbed source of calcium in the Irish diet. One glass of milk is around 300mg. A pot of yoghurt, around 200mg. A matchbox of cheddar, around 250mg.
>
> Not eating dairy? It's absolutely doable, it just has to be deliberate: fortified plant milks (check the label — not all are fortified), tinned sardines with the bones, calcium-set tofu, kale, and almonds.
>
> A vegan diet isn't a bone health problem. An uncounted one is.

**C8-4x.png** — Stat, dark. 4×
> Older skin produces four times less Vitamin D from sunlight than young skin.
>
> Now add Ireland to that. Between October and April, the sun here doesn't sit high enough for your skin to make meaningful Vitamin D at all, whatever age you are.
>
> Which is why for older Irish adults this isn't a summer problem to be solved with more time outdoors. Ask your pharmacist about supplementation.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

**C9-85pc.png** — Stat, cream. 85%
> 85% of Irish adults don't know how much calcium or Vitamin D they should be taking daily.
>
> Not "get it wrong". Don't know. It's not a discipline problem, it's an information problem, and that's a much easier one to fix.
>
> So here it is. Calcium: 1,000mg a day for adults 19 to 50, 1,200mg for women 51+ and everyone 70+. Vitamin D: 600 IU a day up to 70, 800 IU after that. Save this one.
>
> Sources: Empathy Research for Athena, 2023; Institute of Medicine, 2011.

### D — Test & Treat

**D1-risk-factors.png** — Question, dark. Menopause / vegan / steroids
> Going through menopause? Following a vegan diet? Taking steroids long term?
>
> All three raise your risk of osteoporosis, for three different reasons. Menopause removes the oestrogen that protects bone. A vegan diet can be excellent for bone health but takes deliberate calcium planning. Long-term steroids interfere with bone rebuilding directly.
>
> None of these means you'll get osteoporosis. All of them mean it's worth knowing where you stand. The risk check takes two minutes: fragilityfracture.ie/am-i-at-risk
>
> Link in bio.

**D2-dxa.png** — Question, dark. DXA scan
> Should you talk to your GP about a DXA scan?
>
> A DXA scan is a short, painless scan that measures bone density. It's how osteoporosis is actually diagnosed, and it can find bone loss years before a fracture does.
>
> It's worth asking about if you're over 65, if you've had a fracture from a minor fall, if you're on long-term steroids, or if osteoporosis runs in your family.
>
> Read what to expect at fragilityfracture.ie — link in bio.

**D3-fls.png** — Question, yellow. Fracture Liaison Service
> "Should I be referred to a Fracture Liaison Service?"
>
> If you've broken a bone after a minor fall, that's the sentence to bring to your doctor.
>
> A Fracture Liaison Service exists to catch exactly this: people whose first fracture is a warning about their bones, before the second one arrives. Given that 74% of patients aren't on treatment at their first fracture, this question does real work.
>
> Ask it. More at fragilityfracture.ie.

**D4-45pc.png** — Stat, white. 45%
> 62% of Irish adults over 40 buy calcium or Vitamin D supplements. 45% of them dose incorrectly.
>
> Buying it isn't the same as getting it. Wrong strength, wrong frequency, or a formulation that doesn't include the Vitamin D the calcium needs — all common, all invisible.
>
> Bring the bottle to your pharmacist and ask them to check it against your age. It's a two-minute conversation.
>
> Source: Research commissioned by Athena, conducted by Empathy Research, 2023.

### E — Build & Protect

**E1-peak-bone.png** — Stat, cream. 25–30
> You reach your peak bone mass between the ages of 25 and 30.
>
> That's the ceiling. Everything after it is about how slowly you come down from it, which means bone health is one of the few things where the twenty-somethings scrolling past this have more at stake than they think.
>
> If you're past 30: the height of the peak is fixed, but the rate of decline isn't. That part is still yours.
>
> Source: fragilityfracture.ie

**E2-exercise.png** — Quote, cream. Strength work
> Strength and conditioning exercise helps build bone density.
>
> Bone is living tissue and it responds to demand. Load it and it reinforces itself. Don't, and it quietly decides it doesn't need to be that dense.
>
> Which is why walking is good but resistance work is better, and why it's never "too late to start" in the way people assume. Check with your doctor first if you already have a diagnosis, because some movements need modifying.

**E3-early-testing.png** — Hero, dark. Closer
> Early testing and treatment change outcomes.
>
> That's the whole argument. Osteoporosis is silent, it's common, and it's treatable. The only part we can influence is when it gets found.
>
> Everything on fragilityfracture.ie is written in plain English and checked against HSE, NICE and International Osteoporosis Foundation guidance. If one thing in this account made you think about your own bones, the risk check is two minutes.
>
> Link in bio.
